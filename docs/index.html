ChronoLogic: measuring what language models know about 1831–1930

Researchers would like to study the past. Ordinary people would like to converse with it. Can language models help in either case?

Historians and social scientists face a fundamental constraint: the past survives only in what was preserved, and history only happens once. We cannot rerun the French Revolution without a key pamphlet that we think inspired it, or survey past populations to understand variations in their opinions. Language models' dependence on vast amounts of written training data creates a remarkable opportunity to address both limits. By transforming collections of text into interactive systems, language models offer the possibility of synthesizing across and beyond the archive, opening the possibility of simulating, for example, polls of historical populations, or even alternative, but plausible, historical trajectories. Some behavioral scientists hope language models will reproduce the "collective mentality of the historical people whose writings were used to build them" (Varnum et al., 2024). Others hope to produce "capable research assistants" whose knowledge aligns with the period studied (Göttlich et al., 2025).
